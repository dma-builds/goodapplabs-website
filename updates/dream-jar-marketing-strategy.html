Dream in a Jar: Marketing Strategy (after This Is Marketing)

Written by Claude Fable, using the teachings of Seth Godin's This Is Marketing. Shared in Update #8.

The short version

At five, a child will tell you she is going to be an astronaut, a vet and a dragon trainer, all three. By seven, most children have quietly started crossing futures off the list: too hard, not for girls, not for kids like me. Dream in a Jar exists for the parents who want that list to stay long.

The marketing job is not to sell an app. It is to make one behaviour normal among a specific group of parents: when a child names a dream, you help them see themself in it before the world tells them who it is for.

This version starts from the audience and the change. It keeps only the core idea of the product (a child says a dream out loud and sees themself living it) and sets aside the current feature set and every earlier marketing decision.

Who it's for

Door-holder parents: parents of four to eight year olds who believe their job is to keep doors open, not to pick one. Many of them were the kid who was told to be realistic, and they still remember who said it.

What they believe:

  • A child's sense of what is "for someone like me" forms early and mostly out of sight.

  • "You can be anything" is true, and saying it is not enough.

  • A dream does not have to be sensible to deserve respect. Dragon trainer counts.

What they fear is a specific sentence, said casually at the dinner table: "That's for boys." "I'm not smart enough for that." "People like us don't do that." They know that by the time they hear it, the idea has been in there for a while.

What they already do: choose picture books whose heroes look like their kid, take the long way through the museum, answer the fortieth "why" of the day. They are looking for more ways to act on a belief they already hold. We are not persuading anyone of anything.

Who it is not for:

  • Parents building a résumé for a six year old. They want their child ahead; our people want their child unafraid.

  • Parents who want a screen to keep a child quiet.

  • Parents who find a pretend future silly. They are not wrong, they are just not ours.

The change we seek

From children learning what is "for someone like me" from whoever happens to be around them, to children who have already seen themselves there.

The narrowing is real and it is early. In one widely cited study, five year old girls and boys were equally likely to link being "really, really smart" with their own gender; by six, girls were less likely to, and had begun avoiding games described as being for very smart children (Bian, Leslie and Cimpian, Science, 2017). A survey of primary school pupils found the jobs seven year olds pick look much like the ones seventeen year olds pick, and that over a third of children choose from the people they personally know (Drawing the Future, 2018).

So a child's future is being edited years before anyone asks them about it seriously, and the edit is made from a very small sample: family, neighbours, television. The saying usually credited to Marian Wright Edelman is that you can't be what you can't see. Our change is the reverse of it: let them see it first, with their own face in the picture, while everything still feels possible.

Godin's test of culture is "people like us do things like this". Ours:

In families like ours, a child's dream is precious, and we take it seriously.

Seriously does not mean tightly. The dream is the child's to change, outgrow or set down, and we honor that too.

A parent can say that sentence out loud with some pride, and another parent can hear it and think "us too". Everything we make should help someone say it.

The promise

In Godin's template:

  • My product is for people who believe a child should try on every future before the world starts handing out sizes.

  • I will focus on people who want a way to do something about that at home, tonight, in five minutes.

  • I promise that engaging with what I make will help you watch your child see themself as the thing they dreamed up, and learn what they picture for themself while they are still willing to tell you.

The second half of that promise matters as much as the first. The child gets evidence that it could be them. The parent gets a window that closes on its own within a few years.

The story

What we stand against: "be realistic", said too early. Not a company or a competitor, a phrase. Every parent in our audience has had it said to them, and most have caught themselves about to say it.

Where we sit. Take two axes: whose story is it (a character's, or your child's) and what does it do (fill time, or widen what feels possible). Most children's media is a character's story that fills time. The best picture books are a character's story that widens the world. We take the empty corner: your child's own story, widening their world.

How we sound. Like the adult who took you seriously when you were six. Curious, never cute. We ask a child a follow-up question; we do not say "aww". We never rank dreams, and we treat "marine biologist" and "queen of the cloud horses" with the same respect, because the skill being practised is the same one.

What we talk about is children's dreams, in their own words, and what happens when an adult takes one seriously. We almost never talk about the app.

Tension and status

Godin's claim is that people act when they feel tension, and that most decisions are about status. Both are already present here; we name them, we don't invent them.

The tension is a clock. There are roughly three years in which a child will announce any future without checking whether it is allowed. After that they start editing before they speak, and a little later they stop telling their parents at all. A door-holder parent feels this the moment it is pointed out. No countdown timer is needed, and none should ever be used.

The second tension is the gap between saying "you can be anything" and having something to show for it. Our audience says the words weekly and suspects they bounce off.

Status here is affiliation, not dominance. The parent we serve is not asking "is my child ahead?" They are asking "am I the kind of parent who took it seriously?" Everything we make should let them answer yes, and recognise the other parents who do too. The moment a dream becomes something to compare or show off, we have switched games and lost our people.

How it spreads

Start with the smallest group that could carry it. Not "parents". A few hundred door-holders who already gather around the belief: the parents who run the school's career day, kindergarten and first grade teachers who do a "when I grow up" week, children's librarians, communities built around raising confident girls or first-generation kids. These people are already doing the work and are short of good tools. Serve them first.

Give them something worth showing. An idea spreads when passing it on makes the sharer look like who they want to be. A picture of your daughter as the pilot she described at breakfast does that for a parent in a way no app screenshot can. It goes to grandparents, onto the fridge, into the classroom. Each one carries the belief, not an advertisement.

Give the tribe a ritual and a phrase. Tribes have things they do and things they say. Ours could be a dream night once a week, and a question that replaces "what do you want to be when you grow up?", which asks a child to pick one. Ask instead: "What did you dream of being today?" It expects the answer to change.

Earn permission. One short note a week that a door-holder parent would miss if it stopped: a question to ask tonight, a real child's dream in their own words, one thing a parent said that kept a door open. Useful whether or not they ever open the app.

Then be patient. Godin's advice is to show up for the same people long after it gets boring. A belief this size moves family by family, and the parents who join in the first year will do the telling.

What this asks of the product

Godin's order is audience, then change, then product. Taken seriously, this strategy sets a few requirements, whatever the current build does:

  • Every dream is taken seriously. Nothing is corrected, made sensible, or steered toward a career. The dragon trainer gets the same craft as the doctor.

  • The child recognises themself. The whole effect depends on "that's me". A generic cartoon child is a picture book; this has to be a mirror.

  • Range is visible over time. One dream is a nice picture. Forty dreams side by side show a child how many people they have already been, which is the actual point.

  • It gets off the phone. The fridge, a grandparent's wall, a classroom board. A belief needs objects.

  • The parent has a part. A prompt to ask one more question ("what would you do first?") turns a picture into a conversation, and that conversation is the change.

Where to start

  1. Find twenty door-holders and listen. Ask each when they were told to be realistic, and what they have heard their own child rule out. Their words become the copy.

  2. Write the manifesto before the ads. One page that says who this is for, who it is not for, and the sentence about a child's dream being precious. If the right parent reads it and forwards it, the strategy is working before a single install.

  3. Equip one teacher. A first grade "when I grow up" week with every child's dream on the wall puts the idea in front of twenty five families at once, through someone they already trust.

  4. Start the weekly note with those same people and keep it going.

  5. Ask the Godin question after two months: would these families miss it if it were gone? Their answer decides what comes next.
